\section{Appendix: hypotheses reported in full}
\label{app:hypotheses}

This appendix holds the five hypothesis subsections whose verdicts are
summarized in \S\ref{sec:results}. Nothing here is new; it is the same analysis
at the same length, moved so that the main text's argument is carried by the
results that survive it.

\subsection{H1: a cost on proposing selects on intent}
\label{sec:h1}

\emph{Under test.} Mean received intent rises with the signalling cost $c$, and
low-intent proposers exit. Kill condition: the mean intent is flat in $c$.

\emph{Measured.} The direction holds: mean received intent is monotone increasing over the swept grid (Figure~\ref{fig:h1}), so the kill condition is
not met. The figure also shows the real finding. The analytic threshold
$\tau^*(c) = c/(\bar V p)$ is evaluated at a prior success probability, while the
reciprocity the market delivers is a small fraction of that prior. A threshold
evaluated at a probability the market does not supply cuts more aggressively than
the realized one, so the measured points sit between the pooling line and the
analytic curve rather than on it. Selection happens, later and more weakly than
the closed form predicts.

\emph{Scope.} The hypothesis is testable only where the market stays open.
Carrying measured rather than prior reciprocity, the market closes for good a
short way above the bottom of the grid (\texttt{docs/THESIS.md}, H1 caveat;
\texttt{docs/THEORY.md} \S10.2), so the surviving statement is narrower than the
one posed.

\emph{Refuted?} No. Flatness is required and there is none. What is refuted is
the implied magnitude. No interval is registered for this quantity.

\subsection{H2: threshold inflation as defence against pooling}
\label{sec:h2}

\emph{Under test.} The acceptance threshold $\theta$ rises with received volume
\emph{net of} the mean intent of those proposals, and stops rising when $c$ makes
the signal informative. Kill conditions: unrelated to volume, or still rising at
high $c$.

\emph{Measured: there is no instrument.} The shipped $\theta$ update reads one thing, the count of proposals received through clearing, and reads it only as a yield denominator. It never reads received intent. The count is bounded
above by one per proposer while $\theta$ starts far above the unit interval a
yield can occupy, so when the update fires it can only lower $\theta$, for a
minority of acceptors, by a fixed step. No path exists by which a net-of-intent
slope could be positive, because the intent term is not in the code. The observed
decline of $\theta$ in volume is not evidence either way: it reflects which
acceptors the update reaches, and holding reach fixed the association vanishes.

\emph{Would it survive if the mechanism existed?} Partly, and self-defeatingly.
Lengthening the shortlist scales volume but also moves the gate, so it is not a
clean instrument. A flooding term added by hand does produce the predicted sign,
but only at the largest weight tested and the smaller shortlist, and at that weight it
raises the most-swiped acceptors' thresholds until reciprocity falls to
zero and the market closes (\texttt{data/processed/flooding/};
\texttt{docs/THESIS.md}, H2). A defence against pooling that empties the market
is not a defence.

\emph{Refuted?} Formally yes, substantively uninformative: the mechanism is
absent from the model rather than present and ineffective. Reporting this as a
refutation would be a category error and as support would be worse. H2 is
untestable under the shipped rule, and that is the finding.


\subsection{H4: an interior optimum in the institutional comparison}
\label{sec:h4}

\emph{Under test.} Welfare under Environment B exceeds welfare under A for
subgraph sizes above a critical mass and falls below it for small sizes, at fixed
cost. Kill condition: the ordering is monotone in size: B always wins, so the
thin-market penalty does nothing, or always loses (the information gain does
nothing). Welfare is scored on a common basis, with agents B excludes priced at
their counterfactual A outcome.

\emph{Measured.} Survives on one slice, killed on the others. At $c_B = 0.01$
against an oracle A, the optimum is size\_fraction $0.65$ with a residual welfare
difference of $\optWelfare$, $t$ interval $[\optWelfareTLo, \optWelfareTHi]$ (\texttt{docs/STATS.md}
\S1, row 7b), and the ordering there is non-monotone, which is the shape H4 asks
for (Figure~\ref{fig:thin}).

\emph{The selection correction the claim requires.} The optimum is the maximum of
forty cells, so an uncorrected interval on it is not an interval on anything.
Corrected over that family, the Benjamini--Hochberg $q$-value is
$\optWelfareQ$, Holm rejects, and the Bonferroni $t$ interval widens to
$[\optWelfareLo, \optWelfareHi]$ (\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S5.3;
\texttt{multiple\_comparisons.csv}). At roughly fifteen standard errors
($\optWelfareMag / (\optWelfareSd/\sqrt{5}) = \optWelfareT$) the correction is cheap.

\emph{The narrower finding.} Of seven cells where B beats A, only two survive
correction ($0.65$ and $0.50$). The optimum is a two-cell feature, not a region;
the curve beyond those cells is indistinguishable from zero. A caution the
correction does not address: the optimum's standard deviation is the ninth
smallest of the forty, at a little under a third of the median cell's spread, so
it is a cell where five seeds happened to agree, which is why the $t$ interval is the
one to read.

\emph{What the correction does not fix.} Bonferroni and Holm widen an interval for
having searched a family; they do not undo the fact that the cell was selected on
low variance as well as high mean. The interval above is therefore an honest width
around a point that may be biased upward by the selection itself, and the two are
not the same problem.

\emph{The re-estimate on seeds that took no part in the choice.} That objection was
answered in this session rather than deferred. The ten \texttt{size\_fraction}
levels at $c = 0.01$ were re-run on seeds 100--119, at the same gate as the
original, after the prediction was committed
(\texttt{docs/PREREGISTRATION.md} E2; \texttt{data/processed/rigor/c2\_fresh\_inert/}).
Two things happened, and they point opposite ways.

The \emph{location} replicated. On the fresh grid the cell at $0.65$ still ranks
first of ten, with positive values from $0.35$ through $1.00$, a maximum at
$0.65$, and negatives below $0.25$: the non-monotone shape H4 asks for, on twenty
populations that took no part in choosing the cell. Its interval there is
$[\hFourFreshLo, \hFourFreshHi]$, excluding zero ($t = \hFourFreshT$ on 19 df).

The \emph{magnitude and its precision} did not. The fresh estimate is $\hFourFresh$
against $\optWelfare$, and, the larger point, the fresh seed sd is $\hFourFreshSd$
against the original $\optWelfareSd$. The published interval was built on a
standard deviation $\hFourFreshSdRatio$ times too small, because the cell was selected partly for having an unusually
low one. That is the same pathology \texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S2.2 found for
algorithmic loss and clearing, appearing in the cell this hypothesis is built on.

So the correct reading is: an interior optimum at subgraph size $0.65$ that
replicates on fresh seeds, with a gain of roughly $+54$ rather than $+65$, and an
uncertainty the published $t$ interval understates by a factor of about five. A
reader who wants one number should take the fresh one.

\emph{And it is an inert-regime result, which the shipped regime refutes.} The
whole grid above was measured at \texttt{admissible\_fraction} $0.2$, the
configuration \S\ref{sec:h4regime} shows to be deaf to the market's own outcomes;
the shipped value is $0.02$. That experiment was then run: the same ten-by-four
grid at $0.02$ on the same five seeds has \textbf{no cell of the forty beating
Environment A}, at any size and any of the four costs, and at $c = 0.01$ the
difference falls almost monotonically with size from $\hFourShippedSmall$ to
$\hFourShippedFull$. The cell that is the optimum here reads $\hFourShippedOpt$ there.

So H4 as stated is refuted at the configuration the rest of the paper runs, and
what survives is a narrower statement: in a market whose threshold gate never
binds, an interior optimum exists at a subgraph size around $0.65$. A companion
result that requires a configuration the main study does not use is a result
about that configuration, and it should be read that way.

\emph{Killed elsewhere.} At $c_B \geq 0.05$ B always loses: no size beats A
beyond seed noise (\texttt{data/processed/institutional/}). Against an
NCF-mediated A rather than an oracle A the ordering reverses and B always wins,
so the result is about the quality of A's visibility, not subgraph size as such.

\emph{Refuted?} Yes, on the $c_B \geq 0.05$ slice, and yes in that the hypothesis
is a property of one cost level rather than of the model. What survives: at a
cost low enough that entry screens little, an interior optimum exists and is two
cells wide.

\subsection{H5: the decomposition's interaction term}
\label{sec:h5}

\emph{Under test.} The welfare change from an exogenous status shift and from
algorithmic mediation decomposes into additive and interaction parts, with the
interaction estimated rather than assumed. The hypothesis does not predict its
sign; it predicts the term is estimable and may be near zero.

\emph{Measured.} The interaction is $\hFiveInteraction$ with a $t$ interval of
$[\hFiveInteractionLo, \hFiveInteractionHi]$ and a BCa interval of
$[\hFiveInteractionBcaLo, \hFiveInteractionBcaHi]$; its BH $q$-value over the three
terms is $\hFiveInteractionQ$ and it is not rejected (\texttt{docs/STATS.md}
\S5.4). Indistinguishable from zero, as H5 allowed.

\emph{The terms that are not zero.} The algorithmic term is $\hFiveAlgorithmic$
$[\hFiveAlgorithmicLo, \hFiveAlgorithmicHi]$ and dominates: mediation costs an
order of magnitude more welfare than the demographic shift moves it. The exogenous
term is $\hFiveExogenous$, and it is where the two methods disagree: its BCa
interval $[\hFiveExogenousBcaLo, \hFiveExogenousBcaHi]$ excludes zero while its $t$
interval $[\hFiveExogenousTLo, \hFiveExogenousTHi]$ and $q$-value of
$\hFiveExogenousQ$ do not. At five seeds with this skew the BCa interval is optimistic, so the
defensible reading is a negative sign with unresolved magnitude.
Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the arithmetic and the scale difference.

\emph{Refuted?} No. The kill condition was incomparability of the factorial
cells, which would leave the decomposition undefined; comparability is asserted
per seed and holds. The measurement establishes a negative result reported
honestly: drift and mediation act through separate channels here, and the claim
that the algorithm amplifies the shift has no support.

\subsection{H6: differential attrition by intent}
\label{sec:h6}

\emph{Under test.} High-intent agents leave Environment A at a higher rate than
low-intent agents. Kill condition: attrition flat in $\tau$, or the sign
reversed.

\emph{Measured: not at all.} The model has no attrition mechanism. Agents do not
exit in response to outcomes; the only population change is generational
turnover, which is not a decision and does not read $\tau$. No committed artifact
exists for this hypothesis and no quantity is registered, so no number is quoted.

\emph{What can be said without one.} The sign of any measured correlation is
determined by the exit rule imposed, and the two examined point opposite ways:
keying exit on being unmatched yields no association, since matching does not
depend on $\tau$; keying it on net payoff yields a strong positive one, but by cost burn
rather than rejection: high-intent agents propose far more often and
are matched at the same rate (\texttt{docs/THESIS.md}, H6). Neither tests the
hypothesis, which is about a market with an exit margin.

\emph{Refuted?} Neither confirmed nor refuted: untested, and reported as such.
The cost-burn channel it exposes is the same one that kills H4 at high signalling
cost.

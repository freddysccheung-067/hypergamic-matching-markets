\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

The model is a finite two-sided market cleared once per epoch, with a learned
ranker standing between the two sides. Three layers run in a loop: agents form
preferences, a collaborative filter decides what each agent can see, a matching
algorithm clears the market over what was visible, and the clearing outcome
feeds back into the acceptance threshold that gates the next epoch's visibility.
This section describes each layer, the two institutional environments the paper
compares, the utility that drives them, and the condition under which the
feedback channel is live rather than decorative.

\subsection{The agent layer}

Agents are split into a proposer side $M$ and an acceptor side $W$ of equal
size. Each carries a four-dimensional state vector $[s, a, c, t]$: status,
attractiveness, sociability and age. Status is drawn lognormal; the other three
are clipped to the unit interval. Every trait is $z$-scored against population
statistics before it reaches a utility, so each weight below is expressed
against a unit-variance trait and the weights are directly comparable to one
another. The choice to $z$-score is load-bearing for interpretation: it means
only the \emph{rank order} of a trait within the population matters, and a
distribution with no variance would annihilate its term.

Two further agent attributes drive the institutional comparison. A private
\emph{intent} type $\tau_i \in (0,1]$ is each agent's willingness to commit,
invisible to others. A pairwise \emph{alignment} $g_{ij} \in [0,1]$ measures
value compatibility and enters the dissolution hazard rather than the utility.

\subsection{The recommender layer}

A neural collaborative filter with a generalized matrix factorisation path and
an MLP path is trained on the swipe log, the record of which pairs were liked, and
scores every unseen pair. Each agent then keeps a top-$k$
shortlist of length $k = 20$ from those scores, and \emph{nothing outside that
shortlist is visible to the matching step}. This is the architectural commitment
the paper rests on: the filter is not a convenience wrapper around a preference
list, it \emph{is} the preference list. It is also why a preference expressed
over this list is not the preference the same agent holds over the population,
which is the point of departure for the whole exercise.

The ranker is deliberately off-the-shelf. It is cited for the estimator and not
for a result about markets, and the contribution would be void if it depended on
a novel architecture.

\subsection{The matching layer}

Clearing is a truncated deferred-acceptance pass over the shortlists, returning
the matched pairs and a blocking-pair count. Truncation matters: with lists
truncated at $k$, the procedure is the classical algorithm run on top-$k$ lists
rather than on full lists \citep{gale1962}, and the classical stability
guarantee does not carry over unchanged \citep{roth1999}. It also means the
clearing bias is a property of the procedure class and not of the algorithm
specifically, as the wage-forming counterpart of the same procedure shows
\citep{kelso1982}. A full-information arm ranks by true utility under the same
proposer direction and the same shortlist length, and is the benchmark against
which algorithmic loss is measured.

Two properties of the clearing step bound what any $W$-side level can mean.
Deferred acceptance with $M$ proposing returns the proposer-optimal matching,
which is simultaneously the acceptor-pessimal one: among all stable matchings,
every acceptor receives her worst \citep{roth1982,knuth1996}. Since hypergamy
lives in the acceptor's utility, this bias lands on exactly the side the paper
is about. The full-information and mediated arms use the same direction, so the
\emph{gap} between them is far less affected than either absolute level. Any
$W$-side level reported here is a lower bound across stable matchings and must
never be compared against a $W$-proposing baseline without saying so.

\subsection{The utility}

Utility is additive over four terms: a status term, an attractiveness term, a
distance penalty, and an indicator bonus for a status surplus. The bonus is what
makes the model hypergamic rather than merely assortative. It rewards a partner at or
above the agent's own status by a margin $\delta$, and is the
model's operationalisation of the requirement that a partner rank at or above
you rather than beside you.

Two parameters modify the utility's realized shape. A per-agent lognormal
multiplier with coefficient of variation $\sigma_{\text{taste}}$ introduces taste
heterogeneity, so different agents weight the same traits differently, which is the same
device by which idiosyncratic tastes are added to a transferable-utility
marriage model \citep{choo2006}. A persistent pairwise chemistry shock with
standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{shock}}$, drawn once per ordered pair and held
across epochs, enters in absolute utility units. Neither has an empirical
anchor, and \S\ref{sec:sensitivity} treats both as knobs whose value changes the
headline rather than as estimates.

\subsection{The two environments}

Environment A is the global market: a large population, visibility mediated
globally by the learned ranker, no signalling cost, no entry filter.

Environment B is a subgraph of the \emph{same} population. Agents are drawn into
a pool of size at most $\phi N$, filtered on an observable value score, and
within the pool visibility is complete, since every entrant sees every other, so B has
information and no liquidity where A has liquidity and no information.
The comparison is therefore a comparative static in an \emph{institutional}
parameter at a fixed population, which is a different exercise from the
comparative statics in market size that the matching literature already treats
\citep{crawford1991}; changing the subgraph size does move one side's size, and
that channel is separated from the institution effect by holding the population
fixed and varying only the shares.
Proposing in B costs $c_B > 0$ and succeeds with probability $p$, so a
proposal's presence carries information about the proposer's intent. A
defection penalty $d$ is charged to a defector after a match dissolves.

The signalling mechanism is the model's core. An agent proposes only when
$\tau_i V_{ij} p > c$, so the set of proposers is exactly
$\{i : \tau_i > c/(V_{ij}p)\}$, and the separating threshold is
\begin{equation}
\tau^*(c) = \frac{c}{\bar V p}.
\label{eq:tau-star}
\end{equation}
At $c \to 0$ the threshold vanishes, every type proposes, and a received
proposal carries no information about intent: the posterior equals the
population mean. This is a pooling outcome reached through a \emph{cost}
channel rather than through unobservable quality \citep{akerlof1970}, and the
paper treats it as an analogy to the classical lemons result rather than as an
application of it. At $c > 0$ the threshold is positive, low-intent agents exit
by their own incentive to avoid the cost, and the posterior rises: a separating
outcome in the single-crossing sense \citep{spence1973}. A and B are therefore
two settings of one parameter, not two theories.

An agent who cannot infer intent from a proposal must use something else, and
the model's rational response is to raise the bar on observables. That is the
acceptance threshold $\theta$, and its derivation from the pooling result rather
than its assumption as an exogenous contagion is the content of H2.

\subsection{Welfare, and the comparability rule}

Welfare is defined per epoch over the whole population: the value created by
matched pairs, less the signalling cost spent on proposals, less the loss from
dissolved matches including the defection penalty.

The accounting rule is what makes the comparison meaningful. Agents excluded
by B's entry filter are not deleted, and agents who fail to match inside B are
not dropped: every agent appears in the welfare sum exactly once, and an agent B
excludes is scored at whatever A would have given them. Without this rule an
entry filter would look like a gain for the purely arithmetic reason that it
removes the agents it cannot serve. This is a response to a known objection
rather than a safeguard the project devised, since restricting choice can raise the
welfare of those inside the restriction \citep{halaburda2018}, and the model prices the
cost to non-participants instead of ignoring it.

Both accountings are computed. The common one, over all $N$ agents, is the
number the paper reports. The selected one, over entrants only, is computed so
that the selection artifact can be reported honestly rather than hidden, and is
never quoted as a headline.

\subsection{The $\theta$ gate and its binding condition}

The one feedback channel in the model runs from clearing outcomes to
visibility. After each epoch, an acceptor whose received proposal count is
informative updates $\theta$; $\theta$ is the quantile of her own utility
distribution above which a candidate is admissible, so raising $\theta$ shortens
her admissible set and lowering it lengthens it.

The gate binds only under a condition that is worth stating exactly, because
several results below are regime-specific and the regime is set by it. Let
$\phi$ be the target admissible fraction, the share of candidates an agent finds
admissible at epoch 0, and let $k$ be the shortlist length. An agent's
admissible set at epoch 0 has about $\phi N$ members. The gate can only truncate
an agent's visibility if that set is \emph{shorter} than the shortlist, since
otherwise she takes the top $k$ of a larger pool wherever $\theta$ sits and the
update moves nothing. So the gate binds when
\begin{equation}
\phi < \frac{2k}{N}.
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
At the shipped values $k = 20$ and $N = 1000$ this is $\phi < 0.04$. The config
ships $\phi = 0.02$, inside the binding region; the earlier shipped value was
$\phi = 0.2$, outside it, and the ablation data for that regime is labelled
\emph{inert} throughout. The factor of two in \eqref{eq:gate} reflects that the
relevant comparison is against the candidate set on each agent's own side of the
market rather than against the whole population.

Three consequences follow, and they are used later. First, $\phi$ and $k$
interact: raising the shortlist length or lowering the population requires
lowering $\phi$ to keep the gate live, and the configuration validator warns
when the rule is broken. Second, the difference between an inert run and a live
run is a statement about whether the market responds to its own clearing
outcomes, so an ablation measured at $\phi = 0.2$ is not a noisy version of one
measured at $0.02$; it is a different dynamical system. Third, the gate's
liveness is a property of one epoch's calibration, and it relaxes: the update
pulls $\theta$ toward a yield in the unit interval from an initial value far
above it, so the gate truncates at the start and stops truncating within a few
epochs. That relaxation is why the live and inert regimes produce nearly
identical final-epoch gaps despite different trajectories.

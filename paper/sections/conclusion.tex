\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A learned ranker between the two sides of a matching market concentrates
exposure relative to an oracle, and the concentration is a start-up cost rather
than a standing feature. That is the paper's finding, and it is a finding about
\emph{when} the measurement is taken as much as about what is measured: at the
shipped hundred-epoch horizon the gap is $+0.0251$ and looks like a floor; at
three hundred epochs it has decayed to below $0.001$, and the hundred-epoch
figure turns out to be fifty-five times its terminal value. A literature that
reports single-horizon comparisons cannot distinguish a transient from a bias,
and this mechanism supplies a case where the distinction is decisive.

Three qualifications travel with it. The ordering is robust --- positive in
every cell of a twelve-cell sweep --- but its size varies fivefold, so no single
number is "the" gap. The explanation is conditional: the filter learning
coverage accounts for the decay at the shipped setting and cannot account for it
where coverage never climbs, and convergence has been tested at only one point
in parameter space. And the institutional comparison yields an interior optimum
that survives multiplicity correction but is two cells wide, so it is a location
with narrow support rather than a curve with a maximum.

Two hypotheses did not survive as stated and one could not be tested. The
mechanism the second names is absent from the shipped rule, and the model has no
attrition margin for the sixth. Those are reported as findings rather than
omissions, because a six-for-six ledger would be evidence of a design that could
not fail.

What would move this from a model result toward a claim about markets is
calibration, and the paper does not attempt it. Until then the results bound
what this mechanism can do under a stated utility, and the bound is the
contribution.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The six hypotheses are reported one to a subsection. Each opens with the
statement tested, gives the measured result with an interval where one is
registered, and closes by naming what would have refuted it and whether that
happened. The convergence result gets its own subsection because it asks about a
limit rather than testing a hypothesis. Table~\ref{tab:ledger} collects the four
hypotheses with a committed interval; the other two have none, for reasons stated
in place.

Two conventions hold. Every interval is a 95\% interval over the seed
distribution, and table~\ref{tab:ledger} prints both the Student-$t$ and the BCa
bounds for every row rather than choosing one silently, because which is
preferred is a small-sample question and the two disagree at five seeds. The
$t$ interval is the one with nominal coverage at n = 5; the BCa bootstrap attains
roughly 84\% there and returns a half-width narrower than the sample's own
standard deviation (\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S1). At twenty seeds the two agree to
0.002. Where a row's interval has been widened for multiplicity, which is the H4
row only, the caption and the row both say so: the H4 bounds are Bonferroni-$t$
across the 40 institutional cells, and the uncorrected and Bonferroni-BCa bounds
are beside them in the text (\S\ref{sec:h4}; \texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S5.3). Gaps
are always the paired per-seed difference, never a comparison of two arms'
overlapping intervals.

\subsection{H1: a cost on proposing selects on intent}
\label{sec:h1}

\emph{Under test.} Mean received intent rises with the signalling cost $c$, and
low-intent proposers exit. Kill condition: the mean intent is flat in $c$.

\emph{Measured.} The direction holds --- mean received intent is monotone
increasing over the swept grid (Figure~\ref{fig:h1}) --- so the kill condition is
not met. The figure also shows the real finding. The analytic threshold
$\tau^*(c) = c/(\bar V p)$ is evaluated at a prior success probability, while the
reciprocity the market delivers is a small fraction of that prior. A threshold
evaluated at a probability the market does not supply cuts more aggressively than
the realised one, so the measured points sit between the pooling line and the
analytic curve rather than on it. Selection happens, later and more weakly than
the closed form predicts.

\emph{Scope.} The hypothesis is testable only where the market stays open.
Carrying measured rather than prior reciprocity, the market closes for good a
short way above the bottom of the grid (\texttt{docs/THESIS.md}, H1 caveat;
\texttt{docs/THEORY.md} \S10.2), so the surviving statement is narrower than the
one posed.

\emph{Refuted?} No --- flatness is required and there is none. What is refuted is
the implied magnitude. No interval is registered for this quantity.

\subsection{H2: threshold inflation as defence against pooling}
\label{sec:h2}

\emph{Under test.} The acceptance threshold $\theta$ rises with received volume
\emph{net of} the mean intent of those proposals, and stops rising when $c$ makes
the signal informative. Kill conditions: unrelated to volume, or still rising at
high $c$.

\emph{Measured: there is no instrument.} The shipped $\theta$ update reads one
thing --- the count of proposals received through clearing --- and reads it only
as a yield denominator. It never reads received intent. The count is bounded
above by one per proposer while $\theta$ starts far above the unit interval a
yield can occupy, so when the update fires it can only lower $\theta$, for a
minority of acceptors, by a fixed step. No path exists by which a net-of-intent
slope could be positive, because the intent term is not in the code. The observed
decline of $\theta$ in volume is not evidence either way: it reflects which
acceptors the update reaches, and holding reach fixed the association vanishes.

\emph{Would it survive if the mechanism existed?} Partly, and self-defeatingly.
Lengthening the shortlist scales volume but also moves the gate, so it is not a
clean instrument. A flooding term added by hand does produce the predicted sign,
but only at the largest weight tested and the smaller shortlist --- and at that
weight it raises the most-swiped acceptors' thresholds until reciprocity falls to
zero and the market closes (\texttt{data/processed/flooding/};
\texttt{docs/THESIS.md}, H2). A defence against pooling that empties the market
is not a defence.

\emph{Refuted?} Formally yes, substantively uninformative: the mechanism is
absent from the model rather than present and ineffective. Reporting this as a
refutation would be a category error and as support would be worse. H2 is
untestable under the shipped rule, and that is the finding.

\subsection{H3: estimation error concentrates exposure}
\label{sec:h3}

\emph{Under test.} On a shared population and seed, the exposure Gini under the
learned ranker exceeds that under an oracle ranking by true utility, because the
ranker cannot represent every agent and those it cannot represent receive no
exposure. Kill condition: the ranker matches or beats the oracle.

\emph{Measured.} Not refuted. At the shipped regime and horizon the paired gap is
$+0.0251$ with a $t$ interval of $[+0.0155, +0.0347]$, positive on all five
seeds (\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S1, row 2; \texttt{intervals.csv}). The twenty-seed
run gives $+0.0237$ $[+0.0204, +0.0271]$, which is the same quantity on a
superset of the same seeds: seeds 42--46 are the first five of 42--61, so the two
figures agreeing is expected and is not a test of anything. What it does
establish is the interval's width --- 0.0035 on the BCa construction at twenty
seeds against 0.0121 at five --- and that the point estimate moves by less than
its own five-seed interval when the other fifteen seeds are added. A reader who
wants the five-seed accident ruled out should read the sensitivity sweep below,
where the same ordering is measured on twelve independently varied
configurations, rather than the overlapping-sample agreement. The gap is positive
with an interval excluding zero in all twelve sweep cells and reverses nowhere
(\S\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\emph{Refuted?} No in direction; the substantive version --- that estimation
error is a \emph{standing} source of concentration --- is refuted by
\S\ref{sec:coldstart}. The inequality is robust; the mechanism attributed to it
is not.

\emph{What it is not.} The oracle ranks by true utility under the same direction
and shortlist length: an upper bound on what this architecture could achieve, not
a deployable alternative. A gap against an oracle measures how far a learned
ranker falls short, not what a better-designed platform would do.

\subsection{The cold-start result, and its convergence test}
\label{sec:coldstart}

\emph{The question.} H3's gap is positive at every horizon measured. Is it a
floor or a transient? A hundred-epoch run cannot answer, because the gap is still
falling at epoch 99. The run was extended to 300 epochs at the shipped
configuration, five seeds (\texttt{data/processed/rigor/long\_horizon/};
\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S3).

\emph{Trajectory.} The mean gap rises to a peak shortly after the start, then
decays monotonically in the reported points:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrrrr}
\hline
epoch & 0 & 6 & 19 & 49 & 99 & 124 & 199 & 250--299 \\
gap & $+0.2633$ & $+0.3055$ & $+0.2869$ & $+0.1718$ & $+0.0241$
    & $+0.0072$ & $+0.0025$ & $+0.00037$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The peak is at epoch 6. The hundred-epoch figure is not a floor: at epoch 99 the
gap is roughly fifty times the residual it settles at, though both numbers are
imprecise enough that the ratio is an order of magnitude, not a measured
constant (\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S3.2).

Two cautions on reading the table. The points after epoch 124 are all within one
final-epoch seed standard deviation of each other ($0.0111$), so the decay is
established to about epoch 124 and the later entries are ordered noise rather
than a measured continuation: the claim that the series is \emph{monotone} holds
only of the points printed, not of the underlying trajectory. And the entries at
124, 199 and 250--299 are small differences of quantities whose own intervals are
wide relative to their distance from zero, which is why no ratio is quoted as a
measurement.

\emph{Convergence test.} The tail mean over epochs 250--299 is $+0.00037$ with a
95\% $t$ interval of $[+0.000013, +0.000735]$, all five seeds positive
($t = 2.88$, $p = 0.045$; Wilcoxon $p = 0.063$). The BCa interval on the final
epoch is $[-0.00029, +0.00105]$ and includes zero
(\texttt{long\_horizon\_asymptote.csv}). The methods disagree about whether a
residual exists and agree about its size, at most seven parts in ten thousand of
the Gini scale.

\emph{What ``converges'' is doing here, and what it is not.} The word appears
elsewhere in this paper and carries more than the design delivers. A
limit is not observed; a tail is. The measured statement is that the gap is below
$0.001$ from about epoch 250 and is no longer distinguishable from flat at five
seeds. A flat tail is also what a very slow decay looks like, and no test here
separates the two: the stationarity result below cannot, because a ten-epoch
window at five seeds cannot detect a change of a size that would still matter.
The paper therefore reports a bounded residual, not a convergence rate, and does
not quote $+0.00037$ as a floor.

\emph{Stationarity.} A paired test of epochs 290--299 against 280--289 gives
$+0.00010$ ($p = 0.324$; Wilcoxon $0.313$; three of five seeds higher). The
largest single step in the last ten epochs is $0.00041$, or $3.7\%$ of the
final-epoch seed standard deviation (\texttt{long\_horizon\_stationarity.csv}).
The trajectory has stopped resolving above population noise, so 300 epochs
suffices here and 100 does not --- but "stops resolving" is a statement about
the measurement, not about the process.

\emph{The curve fit, reported and not trusted.} AIC and BIC prefer
decay-to-constant over exponential decay decisively ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC} = 23.9$
from the peak), but the constant it estimates is \emph{negative} with an interval
excluding zero while the observed tail is positive: a single exponential cannot
represent a series that first rises, so the constant absorbs the misfit rather
than estimating an asymptote (\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S3.1;
\texttt{long\_horizon\_decay\_fits.csv}). The comparison answers which curve fits
better, not what the asymptote is.

\emph{What this establishes.} That the exposure gap at the shipped configuration
is a cold-start property that disappears, and that a hundred-epoch figure
describes a transient. Not that the mechanism is the one H3 offers
(\S\ref{sec:sensitivity} finds a cell where coverage never climbs and the gap
closes anyway), nor that convergence holds elsewhere, since the 300-epoch run
exists only at the shipped cell.

\subsection{H4: an interior optimum in the institutional comparison}
\label{sec:h4}

\emph{Under test.} Welfare under Environment B exceeds welfare under A for
subgraph sizes above a critical mass and falls below it for small sizes, at fixed
cost. Kill condition: the ordering is monotone in size --- B always wins (the
thin-market penalty does nothing) or always loses (the information gain does
nothing). Welfare is scored on a common basis, with agents B excludes priced at
their counterfactual A outcome.

\emph{Measured.} Survives on one slice, killed on the others. At $c_B = 0.01$
against an oracle A, the optimum is size\_fraction $0.65$ with a residual welfare
difference of $+64.62$, $t$ interval $[+52.37, +76.86]$ (\texttt{docs/STATS.md}
\S1, row 7b), and the ordering there is non-monotone, which is the shape H4 asks
for (Figure~\ref{fig:thin}).

\emph{The selection correction the claim requires.} The optimum is the maximum of
forty cells, so an uncorrected interval on it is not an interval on anything.
Corrected over that family, the Benjamini--Hochberg $q$-value is
$3.9\times10^{-4}$, Holm rejects, and the Bonferroni $t$ interval widens to
$[+28.80, +100.43]$ (\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S5.3;
\texttt{multiple\_comparisons.csv}). At roughly eleven standard errors the
correction is cheap.

\emph{The narrower finding.} Of seven cells where B beats A, only two survive
correction ($0.65$ and $0.50$). The optimum is a two-cell feature, not a region;
the curve beyond those cells is indistinguishable from zero. A caution the
correction does not address: the optimum's standard deviation is the ninth
smallest of the forty, at a little under a third of the median cell's spread, so
it is a cell where five seeds happened to agree --- which is why the $t$ interval
is the one to read.

\emph{Killed elsewhere.} At $c_B \geq 0.05$ B always loses: no size beats A
beyond seed noise (\texttt{data/processed/institutional/}). Against an
NCF-mediated A rather than an oracle A the ordering reverses and B always wins,
so the result is about the quality of A's visibility, not subgraph size as such.

\emph{Refuted?} Yes, on the $c_B \geq 0.05$ slice, and yes in that the hypothesis
is a property of one cost level rather than of the model. What survives: at a
cost low enough that entry screens little, an interior optimum exists and is two
cells wide.

\subsection{H5: the decomposition's interaction term}
\label{sec:h5}

\emph{Under test.} The welfare change from an exogenous status shift and from
algorithmic mediation decomposes into additive and interaction parts, with the
interaction estimated rather than assumed. The hypothesis does not predict its
sign; it predicts the term is estimable and may be near zero.

\emph{Measured.} The interaction is $+16.70$ with a $t$ interval of
$[-33.42, +66.82]$ and a BCa interval of $[-13.30, +53.05]$; its BH $q$-value
over the three terms is $0.407$ and it is not rejected (\texttt{docs/STATS.md}
\S5.4). Indistinguishable from zero, as H5 allowed.

\emph{The terms that are not zero.} The algorithmic term is $-717.61$
$[-795.77, -651.37]$ and dominates: mediation costs an order of magnitude more
welfare than the demographic shift moves it. The exogenous term is $-21.10$, and
it is where the two methods disagree --- its BCa interval $[-41.13, -3.42]$
excludes zero while its $t$ interval $[-52.53, +10.32]$ and $q$-value of $0.204$
do not. At five seeds with this skew the BCa interval is optimistic, so the
defensible reading is a negative sign with unresolved magnitude.
Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the arithmetic and the scale difference.

\emph{Refuted?} No. The kill condition was incomparability of the factorial
cells, which would leave the decomposition undefined; comparability is asserted
per seed and holds. The measurement establishes a negative result reported
honestly: drift and mediation act through separate channels here, and the claim
that the algorithm amplifies the shift has no support.

\subsection{H6: differential attrition by intent}
\label{sec:h6}

\emph{Under test.} High-intent agents leave Environment A at a higher rate than
low-intent agents. Kill condition: attrition flat in $\tau$, or the sign
reversed.

\emph{Measured: not at all.} The model has no attrition mechanism. Agents do not
exit in response to outcomes; the only population change is generational
turnover, which is not a decision and does not read $\tau$. No committed artefact
exists for this hypothesis and no quantity is registered, so no number is quoted.

\emph{What can be said without one.} The sign of any measured correlation is
determined by the exit rule imposed, and the two examined point opposite ways:
keying exit on being unmatched yields no association, since matching does not
depend on $\tau$; keying it on net payoff yields a strong positive one, but by
cost burn rather than rejection --- high-intent agents propose far more often and
are matched at the same rate (\texttt{docs/THESIS.md}, H6). Neither tests the
hypothesis, which is about a market with an exit margin.

\emph{Refuted?} Neither confirmed nor refuted: untested, and reported as such.
The cost-burn channel it exposes is the same one that kills H4 at high signalling
cost.

\subsection{Robustness across the parameter sweep}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The hypotheses above were each measured at one setting of four modelling knobs.
This subsection asks where they hold --- which is why the paper can report a size
for the exposure gap at all, since one cell cannot separate a property of the
mechanism from a property of its corner. Twelve cells, one knob moving at a time,
four claims fixed with their thresholds before the cells were read
(Table~\ref{tab:sens}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{The sensitivity grid: one knob at one level per row, the rest at shipped
values, five seeds, 100 epochs. Gap is the paired exposure difference and the

appears in three axes with identical numbers, which checks that the axes are

claim fails.}
\label{tab:sens}

\emph{Holds everywhere.} The H3 ordering holds in all twelve cells, positive with
an interval excluding zero, reversing nowhere (C1). The gap is small by the
pre-fixed ceiling in all twelve (C2), though the largest cell's upper bound
reaches $+0.0665$ and is the weakest case. Clearing is non-degenerate in all
twelve (C4). Neither the ordering nor the existence of a clearing market is an
artefact of the shipped corner.

\emph{What does not.} One cell fails the reach claim: at the lowest chemistry
noise the ranker reaches $0.4616$ of agents against $0.9566$ shipped (C3). This
is the mechanism switching off, not a small deviation: at low chemistry noise the
filter never learns coverage, so the process \S\ref{sec:coldstart} identifies as
the reason the gap closes is unavailable. The gap is still small and positive
there ($+0.0097$), but because \emph{both} arms concentrate and the ranker has not
caught up.

This is a conclusion reversing. The cold-start explanation is conditional: it
requires the filter to learn whom it can represent. The convergence in
\S\ref{sec:coldstart} is therefore a fact about the shipped configuration, not a
general property of the mechanism, and whether the gap converges at the cell where
reach fails is untested.

\emph{How large.} The gap ranges from $+0.0097$ to $+0.0470$, a factor of about
five, with no change in sign; the shipped cell sits mid-range. Quote the range
with its condition, or the shipped cell with its regime, never $+0.025$ as
``the'' gap.

\emph{A documented condition that does not reproduce.} The configuration states,
and a guard test pins, that the hypergamy margin $\delta$ has power only when
$\beta/\alpha \geq 2$. Re-measuring shortlist overlap at the shipped
heterogeneity and noise reverses the ordering: $\beta/\alpha = 1$ gives $\delta$
\emph{more} power than $3$, and all three levels clear the threshold the
guardrail treats as meaningful (\texttt{docs/STATS.md} \S4.2;
\texttt{sensitivity/delta\_power.csv}). The guard test is not wrong; it was
measured on a noise-free homogeneous population, and the rule of thumb describes
that population rather than the one the experiments run on.

What the sweep does show is that $\delta$ moves no headline: the gap is $+0.0229$
at $0$, $+0.0260$ at $0.5$ and $+0.0263$ at $1.0$, a spread of $0.0034$ against
intervals about $0.012$ wide, while the weight ratio moves it twice as far and
chemistry noise four times as far. The defensible statement is that no claim here
rests on $\delta$ at any weight ratio tested --- not that the results require
$\beta/\alpha \geq 2$.

\emph{Refuted?} The sweep refutes the unconditional form of the cold-start
explanation and the stated $\beta/\alpha$ condition. It does not refute H3's
ordering, the one result that survives every cell.

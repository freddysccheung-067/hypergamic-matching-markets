\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a recommender sits between the two sides of a matching market, it decides
which candidates anyone ever sees. The consequence is easy to state and awkward
to price: a preference expressed over a truncated list is not the same
preference that operates over the whole population. The agent on nobody's list
is not rejected; she is never considered, and no preference data will record the
difference. This paper asks what happens to a matching market under that
arrangement, and how it compares with a differently designed market that trades
liquidity for information.

For a single scalar notion of status the aggregate questions have closed-form
answers, and they have recently been settled. When unions must be hypergamous, that is,
when a partner must rank at or above rather than beside, the minimal unmatched mass is a
functional of the two marginal status distributions alone,
and no behavioural strategy changes it. Two things the literature treated as
substantive, the feasibility of a status requirement and the size of the
resulting deficit, turn out to be properties of histograms, and any new work in
that setting is at best a check on a theorem.

This paper works where that theorem does not reach. The constraint here is
\emph{visibility}, not compatibility: the feasible set is the top-$k$ of an
estimated multi-attribute utility, lists differ by agent, and estimator error is
correlated in a way no marginal captures. The closed-form result relies on the
constraint being a nested upper set, which is exactly what makes the deficit a
one-dimensional supremum. Truncated lists from a learned ranker are not nested,
and their error depends on how long the ranker has trained. The structure that
makes the deficit tractable is absent rather than merely harder, so the question
must be answered numerically.

The answer depends on \emph{when} you ask. That is the paper's central finding
and its central methodological warning.

\subsection*{What was done}

A finite two-sided market of a thousand agents carries a four-dimensional trait
vector and a private commitment type. A neural collaborative filter trained on
the swipe log produces a top-20 shortlist per agent, and a truncated
deferred-acceptance pass clears the market over those shortlists and nothing
else. In parallel, a second institution is modelled as a subgraph of the same
population, with complete visibility inside it and a positive signalling cost:
information instead of liquidity, where the global market has liquidity instead
of information. Welfare is scored on a common basis, so agents the subgraph
excludes are priced at their counterfactual outcome rather than deleted, since otherwise
an entry filter improves its own arithmetic by removing the agents it
cannot serve.

Six hypotheses were posed in advance, each with a stated kill condition, and four
kinds of test applied: seed-bootstrapped intervals, a horizon extended to
convergence, a twelve-cell parameter sweep with its claim thresholds fixed before
the cells were read, and multiplicity correction over the forty institutional
cells the headline optimum was selected from. \S\ref{sec:design} states the
sample sizes so the numbers can be checked.

\subsection*{What was found}

\textbf{Estimation error concentrates exposure, and the concentration is a
transient.} The learned ranker's exposure Gini exceeds an oracle's by $+0.0251$
at the shipped horizon, positive on all five seeds. Extending the run to 300
epochs shows this is not a floor but a decay: the gap peaks shortly after the
start, is $+0.0241$ at epoch 99, and is below $0.001$ from about epoch 250,
where it stops being distinguishable from flat at five seeds. That is a bounded
residual rather than a demonstrated limit, because a flat tail is also what a very slow
decay looks like, and this design cannot separate the two. A hundred-epoch
measurement of this quantity describes a transient, and every number quoted at
that horizon carries it.

\textbf{The concentration is real everywhere, but its size is not constant.} The
gap is positive with an interval excluding zero in all twelve sweep cells and
reverses nowhere, while its magnitude varies fivefold. A reader should quote the
range with its condition, or the shipped cell with its regime, never one number
as ``the'' gap.

\textbf{The explanation is conditional.} The mechanism offered, the filter learning whom it can represent, holds where coverage climbs and fails in the
one cell where it does not. There the gap is also small, but because both arms
concentrate rather than because the ranker caught up. Convergence to zero is
established at one point in parameter space, not as a general property of
mediation.

\textbf{An interior institutional optimum exists, and it is narrow.} At a
signalling cost low enough that entry screens little, welfare under the subgraph
institution exceeds the global oracle's by $+64.62$ at subgraph size $0.65$, and
survives correction for the forty cells it was chosen from. Only two cells
survive that correction, so the optimum is a two-cell feature rather than a
region.

Three of six hypotheses are qualified. One is untestable because the mechanism
it names is absent from the shipped rule, one holds at a single cost level, and
one cannot be tested at all because the model has no attrition margin. They are
reported at the same length as the results that worked, because a paper that
reports only its successes reads as advocacy.

\subsection*{Why it matters, and what it does not claim}

That mediation's effect is horizon-dependent is absent from the recommender-loop
literature, which predicts homogenisation that persists or compounds, and from
the closed-form literature, which has no horizon argument. Had the effect been
measured only at the shipped horizon, or at twenty epochs as an earlier version of this
work was, the paper would have reported a standing deficiency
of learned rankers where the truth is a start-up cost. The general lesson is that
a single-horizon inequality cannot distinguish a standing bias from a transient,
and that the distinction is measurable.

Nothing here is a claim about a population. Every parameter is a modelling
choice, nothing is calibrated, five seeds carry every headline, and one
population size carries it. The results bound what this mechanism can do under a
stated utility; \S\ref{sec:limits} says so at length rather than in a footnote.
